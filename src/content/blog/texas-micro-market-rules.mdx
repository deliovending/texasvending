**Short answer:** Texas treats a workplace micro market as a "self-service food market," a type of retail food establishment, under 25 TAC § 228.225. The rule allows an unstaffed market if it has 24/7 video surveillance with 14 days of records, a responsible party for inspections, labeled and tamper-evident packaging, coolers that stop sales if they fail, and a contact sign at the kiosk. The market operator carries these duties. The host business provides the space.

If your company is weighing a micro market, legal or risk teams will ask whether an unstaffed food store is allowed on site. In Texas it is, and the state has written rules for it. This post walks through each requirement in plain English. It is general information, not legal advice.

## What Is a Self-Service Food Market in Texas?

The Texas Department of State Health Services (DSHS) describes a self-service food market as an unstaffed market that sells prepackaged food, including refrigerated or frozen food that needs temperature control. Shoppers pick items from open racks or coolers, scan them at a kiosk, and pay. No employee is present.

DSHS says these markets are meant for **closed locations**: moderately secured workplaces serving a known group of employees, away from heavy public traffic. That is exactly the setting of an office, warehouse, or plant break room.

## What Does 25 TAC § 228.225 Require?

### Video surveillance

The market must keep **24/7 video surveillance records** of shoppers viewing, selecting, handling, and buying products. Records are kept for **14 calendar days** and made available to the regulatory authority.

### A responsible party

The operator must tell the regulatory authority who will be available for routine inspections.

### Labeled, tamper-evident packaging

Prepackaged food must meet the Food Code labeling rules and be **tamper evident**. Food past its date, missing a date mark, or held outside time and temperature limits must be thrown out.

### Coolers that stop sales when they fail

Refrigerated display units for temperature-sensitive food need an **automatic shut-off**, or a plan approved by the regulatory authority, that stops sales during a power or equipment failure until the unit is serviced. Units must have a **self-closing door** or, if open, hold food at **41°F or below**.

### A contact sign at the kiosk

A sign **visible from the payment kiosk** must list the business to contact with complaints, its address, and its phone number and email or website.

### No person in charge on site

A market that follows § 228.225 does **not** need a person in charge on site during operating hours.

## What Else Does DSHS Say?

The DSHS FAQ adds practical points:

- **Licensing.** These markets are retail food establishments, so the local regulatory authority may charge a license fee. DSHS says they should be classified as low risk. Plan review happens only where the local jurisdiction requires it.
- **No sinks required.** Because no food is prepared and no utensils are washed on site, a hand sink and a three-compartment sink are not required.
- **Produce.** Whole produce such as bananas or grapes may be sold only if it is ready to eat: washed and prepackaged for retail.

Rules and local practice change. Check with your city or county health department for local permit steps.

## What Does the Host Business Do?

In a typical full-service program, the operator owns the equipment, buys and labels the food, runs the cameras, keeps the records, and handles inspections. The host business:

1. **Provides the space**, a power outlet, and access for service visits.
2. **Keeps the market in a closed area** used by employees and regular visitors, not a public lobby.
3. **Asks the vendor** how each requirement above is met, and gets the answers in writing.
